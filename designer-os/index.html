Retro Glitch — the design system

One Figma file is the source of truth for every colour, type style, spacing step and component we own. It is not a mood board and not a suggestion. If a value isn't in the file, it doesn't exist yet.

HOW THE SYSTEM IS ORGANISED

Surface
Grounds and panels. Sets the mood before anything else lands.
Ink
Type and icons. Three steps: primary, secondary, disabled.
Accent
One hero colour. A scalpel, not a paint roller.
Signal
Pass, warn, fail. Semantic — never borrowed for decoration.
Grid
4px base, 8px rhythm. Spacing is a token too.

Live hex values, type ramp and font pairings sit in the Figma file — read them there, never from a screenshot or from this page.

THE SYSTEM IS THE FLOOR

  • Build from published components and variables, every time.
  • Need something that doesn't exist? Design it properly, then propose it into the library.
  • Detach only when you have a reason you'd say out loud in a review.
  • Name layers like someone else will open the file. They will.

NEVER, WITHOUT A CONVERSATION

  • A colour that isn't a variable. "Close enough" hexes are how brands die.
  • A new typeface, or a fifth weight of an existing one.
  • Custom spacing off the 4px grid because it "looked better".
  • A component copy-pasted from another file and quietly edited.
0x03

Seven house laws

If you remember nothing else from this document, remember law one. Everything else is a consequence of it.

01

One hand, many files.

A blog header, an ABM ad and a product empty state should look like the same person made them on the same afternoon. Nobody outside design should be able to tell where one designer stopped and another started. Consistency is not a constraint on your taste — it is the taste.

02

The system is the floor, not the ceiling.

Retro Glitch tells you what's allowed. It doesn't tell you what's good. Stay inside it and still make the best thing on the page.

03

Type is hierarchy, not decoration.

Two families maximum per artefact. Three weights. If you need a fourth to make it work, the layout is the problem, not the type.

04

Colour carries meaning.

Accent means "look here". Signal colours mean pass, warn, fail — and nothing else, ever. The moment green is decorative, green stops meaning green.

05

Contrast is not negotiable.

4.5:1 for body, 3:1 for large text and meaningful UI edges. Our audience reads on bad monitors in bright offices. Check it, don't eyeball it.

06

Steal structure, never skin.

Take how Linear sequences a page or how Stripe explains a hard idea. Never take their colours, their type or their illustration style. Reference is a thinking tool; resemblance is a failure.

07

Ship the honest version on time.

A good asset today beats a perfect one after the campaign ran. Ship, watch how it performs, improve the template for next time.

0x04

Workloads

Seven kinds of work will land on your desk. Open each one — what it is, what "good" means, and the specs so you never have to ask.

04.1 The design brief — how work starts ▼

No work starts without a brief, and you are allowed — expected — to send one back. A brief we can act on names: the audience, the single message, where it will appear, the deadline, and how we'll know it worked.

If a request arrives as "can you make a graphic for this blog", that's not a brief. Ask the two questions that unblock everything: who is reading it, and what do you want them to do next? Two minutes of asking saves two days of revisions.

Audience · who, and how technical Message · one sentence, not five Placement · exact surface and size Success · the metric or the feeling
04.2 Product design — screens and flows ▼

The hardest and most valuable work here. Our products show enormous amounts of data to people who are debugging under pressure. Density is a feature; clutter is not. Your job is to make a 400-row test run scannable in three seconds.

The rule: never design a screen for a workflow you cannot describe out loud. Sit with an engineer, watch a real run, then open Figma. Design the failure state before the happy path — in a testing product, failure is the happy path. And for agentic surfaces, always answer three questions on screen: what is the agent doing, how far along is it, and how do I stop it?

Start · existing components States · empty, loading, partial, error, success Handoff · specs, tokens, edge cases in the file
04.3 Web pages — product, feature, launch, legacy ▼

Four different jobs wearing similar clothes. Product pages convince someone the category matters and we own it. Feature pages assume they're already convinced and answer "how exactly". Launch pages buy attention for a week and are allowed to be louder than everything else we make. Legacy pages are the quiet majority — when you touch one, bring it up to system, don't redesign it into an island.

Every page: the product visible above the fold, one primary action, and a mobile layout designed rather than inherited. Half our traffic reads us on a phone between meetings.

04.4 Marketing design — blog and social imagery ▼

High volume, high repetition. This is where "one hand, many files" is won or lost, because it's the work most likely to be made fast. Build the template properly once, then work inside it — a blog header should be a fifteen-minute job, not a fresh design problem.

Blog images earn the click without lying about the article. Use the product, a diagram, or bold type. Skip the stock photography of people pointing at laptops — our reader can smell it and it costs us credibility we can't buy back.

Blog hero / OG · 1200×630 LinkedIn single · 1200×627 Square · 1080×1080 Portrait · 1080×1350 Text · legible at thumbnail size
04.5 Carousels — LinkedIn and social ▼

Our best-performing organic format, and the one most often done badly. A carousel is a story with a hook, not a deck with a cover. Slide one earns the swipe or nothing else matters; slide two must reward it immediately.

One idea per slide. If a slide needs three sentences to make sense, it's two slides. Number them so people know where they are, keep a consistent left-edge so the swipe feels like motion rather than a jump-cut, and end with one clear next step.

Size · 1080×1350 (4:5) Length · 8–12 slides Export · PDF, fonts embedded Min type · 24px equivalent Safe margin · 64px
04.6 ABM ads and case-study PDFs ▼

ABM means one named company is the audience. The design can and should feel bespoke — their logo, their industry, their words — while staying unmistakably ours. Personal, not cheap.

Case studies live or die on numbers. Find the one figure that makes an engineering leader sit up (runtime cut by 70%, releases weekly instead of monthly) and build the whole layout around it. Treat the PDF as a designed document with a real grid and running hierarchy, not a slide deck exported sideways. It gets printed, forwarded and read on a phone — design for all three.

PDF · A4 + US Letter Images · 300 ppi Fonts · embedded, always Weight · under 10 MB Ads · 1200×628, 1080×1080, 300×250, 728×90
04.7 Motion graphics ▼

Watch the TestMu AI channel before you animate anything — that's the house tempo. Motion here has one job: make an abstract thing (parallel execution, an agent reasoning) legible in seconds.

Craft rules: ease everything, nothing linear except a loading bar. Animate the fewest properties that tell the story — usually opacity and position. Type holds still while it's being read. Cut on the beat of the voiceover, not the music. And build for sound-off first: 85% of feed views are muted, so if it doesn't work silently with captions, it doesn't work.

Study the best design work in AI and B2B SaaS — deliberately, weekly, with a purpose. Each of these is on the list for one specific reason. Take the reason, not the pixels.

HOW TO USE THIS LIST

Reference is not resemblance. You are borrowing a decision, not a look.

"Linear stacks its feature sections in escalating specificity" is a usable reference. "Make it look like Linear" is not — and anything that arrives in review looking like another company's brand goes back, however beautiful it is. Everything you take gets rebuilt in Retro Glitch before anyone else sees it.

0x06

Your toolkit — and the upskilling clause

You get professional licences on day one. In return, there's an expectation: you get better at these tools every single day. We build a testing platform for the AI era — a design team that works like it's 2019 would be an embarrassing look.

PRO LICENCE

Claude

Thinking partner, copywriter, critic. Use it to pressure-test concepts, generate the fifteen headline variants you'd never write alone, and audit your own work before review.

Never let it choose. Judgement stays yours.

PRO LICENCE

Figma

Where the truth lives. Get fluent in variables, auto-layout and component properties — not just drawing rectangles. Speed here compounds across every other task.

If you're still hand-nudging spacing, learn the system.

PRO LICENCE

Midjourney

Texture, atmosphere, exploration. Excellent for moodboards, backgrounds and abstract material.

Not for anything factual, and never for product UI.

AI EARNS ITS KEEP HERE

  • Twenty concept directions in the time you'd sketch three.
  • Copy variants, alt text, and the first draft of a case-study narrative.
  • Ruthless critique of your own layout before a human sees it.
  • Resizing a campaign across nine formats without losing the evening.
  • Learning a tool you don't know yet, at your own pace, tonight.

AI DOES NOT GET TO DO THIS

  • Make the final call. That's what you were hired for.
  • Invent a statistic, a customer quote or a capability we don't ship.
  • Generate anything that lands in front of a customer unedited.
  • Produce imagery of real people, or of competitor products.
  • Replace looking at the actual product with imagining it.
THE DAILY MINIMUM

Thirty minutes a day. One new technique, one new tool, or one piece of great work pulled apart to find out why it works.

Nobody will check. It shows up in your work within a month either way.

0x07

Your first thirty days

You'll get access to templates, live product files, the brand kit and the full asset archive. Here's the order to open them in.

DAY 1–2 · MOUNT THE DRIVES

Licences and access: Figma, Claude, Midjourney, the shared drive, the asset archive. Open Retro Glitch and read it like a book — every page, including the ones nobody links to. Then read this document again; more of it will make sense.

DAY 3–5 · USE THE PRODUCT

Run a real test. Break it deliberately. Sit with someone from QA or engineering for an hour. Write down every word you didn't understand — that list is the vocabulary of your next two years.

WEEK 2 · COPY BEFORE YOU CREATE

Rebuild three existing assets from the archive — a blog header, a carousel, one product screen — without opening the originals' layers. Compare after. The gaps are your onboarding curriculum, and nobody has to tell you what they are.

WEEK 3 · SHIP SOMETHING SMALL

Take a real brief end to end. Small scope, real deadline, real publication. Ask for a proper critique and take notes rather than defending. First ships are meant to be small and finished, not large and nearly.

WEEK 4 · EARN AN OPINION

You've now seen enough to be useful about what's broken. Bring one improvement to the system or a template — with a proposal, not just a complaint. This is the moment you stop being new.
